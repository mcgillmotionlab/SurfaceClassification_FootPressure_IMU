\abstract{(1) Background: Navigating surfaces during outdoor walking can significantly alter biomechanical gait patterns and contribute to fall risk. This study aims to develop and validate tools for automatic walking condition classification using inertial measurement unit (IMU) and foot pressure sensor data. We compared sensor modalities (IMU, pressure insoles, and combined) and evaluated whether gait cycle segmentation improves model performance.
(2) Methods: Twenty participants walking under five conditions (flat, stairs up, stairs down, slope up, and slope down) wearing a suit comprised of seventeen IMUs and shoes fit with pressure insoles. Machine Learning (ML; Extreme Gradient Boosting) and Deep Learning (DL; Convolutional Neural Network) models were trained to classify these conditions.
(3) Results: IMU-based models outperformed insole-only models. The best ML setup (IMU +  insoles, gait segmentation) achieved an F1-score of 0.87, while the best DL setup (IMU + pressure insoles, sliding window) reached 0.90. 
(4) Conclusions: Combining IMU and insole data yielded modest improvements over IMU alone. Gait segmentation slightly enhanced ML performance, whereas DL models performed better with sliding window segmentation. IMU sensors provided the most discriminative features, supporting their use in practical, automatic walking condition classification in unconstrained environments.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Walking in the natural and built outdoor environment is a common task for most individuals that requires navigating various surface topologies such as stairs, ramps, or irregular surfaces.  
From previous work we know that surface topology alters biomechanical gait patterns~\cite{ippersiel_impact_2022, Dixon_2018, dixon_late-cueing_2018, vieira_gait_2017, menz_acceleration_2003, emmerzaal_non-linear_2025}. Recent work has shown that even in healthy, young adults, outdoor walking surfaces elicit biomechanical adaptations~\cite{emmerzaal_non-linear_2025, ippersiel_impact_2022}.
These adaptations could be challenging for aging populations and individuals with gait or mobility issues. In fact, uneven surfaces have been identified as a major factor in outdoor falls among middle-aged and older adults~\cite{li_2006}. %Moreover, Bergland et al.~\cite{Bergland_1998} reported that 59\% of falls in older adults in Norway occurred outdoors, while in Canada fall incidence rates between indoor and outdoor environments were nearly equal at 20-21\%~\cite{Oloughlin_f1994}.
As such, the ability to incorporate outdoor environments in biomechanical analysis would allow us to fully capture the demands of walking. Accordingly, valid and easy-to-use algorithms to classify walking conditions for remote monitoring of real-life behavior are required.

Previous studies have explored the use of various sensor modalities for walking surface classification using Machine Learning (ML) and/or Deep Learning (DL) approaches. A study by Laschowski et al.~\cite{Laschowski_2022} using a smartphone secured to participant's truck via a harness identified surfaces such as stairs up and down, level ground, and surface transitions from video data using a DL convolutional neural networks (CNN)~\cite{Laschowski_2022}. While promising, this approach presents severe privacy implications for daily life use.
% ; however, they did not include other relevant outdoor surface features such as irregular or sloped surfaces. 
%Follow-up work by Whipps et al. (CITATION XXX) integrated a larger number of surfaces and demonstrated that additional LiDar sensor data, available in many latest-generation smartphones, improved prediction accuracy from 82 to 92\% for image only models vs models trained on both image and Lidar data. 
The most popular approach to date, however, has been the development of predictive algorithms from inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors worn on body segments. Multiple studies have implemented algorithms capable of classifying outdoor walking surfaces with high accuracy ($>$ 0.9)~\cite{Shah_2022, chauhan2025classifying, yildiz2023towards, Hu_2021}. Although these approaches are powerful, using multiple body-worn IMU may result in compliance issues during real-world monitoring. It remains unknown if algorithms based on other minimally obtrusive sensors, such as pressure insoles, could provide strong walking condition prediction capabilities. Moreover, many previous surface classification studies relied on the data set of Luo et al.~\cite{Luo_2020} collected using short individual walking bouts, suggesting that the ecological validity of these models has yet to be established. Models need to be trained on data more representative of unconstrained outdoor walking.   
% It remains unknown if ML algorithms based on other minimally obtrusive sensors (i.e., pressure insoles) and minimal number of features could provide strong walking condition prediction capabilities. Moreover, models performance during continuous, unconstrained walking that contain twist and turns should be investigated. 

Additionally, the effect of data segmentation on model performance is largely unknown. Previous work used either segmentation on gait cycles (c.f.~\cite{Shah_2022}) or no segmentation at all (c.f.~\cite{chauhan2025classifying}). As walking condition can influence biomechanics and thus the signal representation within different steps, it is crucial to explore whether data segmentation methods matter. There are two common methods, first, the traditional biomechanical gait cycle segmentation, and second a more data-driven sliding window approach. Segmentation based on gait cycles requires domain knowledge and identification of gait events, whereas fixed window segmentation requires no domain knowledge and would make resulting models independent of gait event identification algorithms. 

Therefore, the goal of this study is to develop and validate tools for automatic walking condition classification using IMU sensor and foot pressure data. The primary aim is to compare the performance of different sensor modalities (IMU, pressure insoles, and combined) in identifying walking condition. The secondary aim is to determine whether gait cycle, compared to fixed window, segmentation improves model performance. We hypothesize that (1) IMU sensors will provide more discriminative information than pressure insoles, with models combining IMU and pressure insole enhancing performance; and (2) gait cycle segmentation will outperform sliding window segmentation. By addressing these research aims, this study contributes to enhancing the ecological validity of ML and DL models in classifying surface topology, thereby aiding biomechanical analysis to occur unconstrained during outdoor walking.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:Methods}

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Segmentation}
\subsubsection{Data Acquisition}
We leveraged  a pre-existing dataset by Losing \& Hasenj{\"a}ger (2022)~\cite{losing_2022} in this study. Briefly, the dataset represents a motion capture experiment that recorded data using Xsens motion capture suit consisting of 17 IMU sensors \cite{xsens} and IEE ActiSense Smart Footwear Sensor pressure insoles \cite{foot_pressure} as participants walked across real-world environments. The data were collected from 20 participants (5 females, ages 18-69 years) walking 3 courses of approximately 500m (Course A, B, C) where they encountered various walking conditions. Data were provided at the downsampled rate of 60 Hz. For additional details, see the original publication (c.f.~\cite{losing_2022}).

\subsection{Data Preparation}
For this study, we reduced the dataset from seven to five classes that were annotated in the database: straight paths, walking up stairs, walking down stairs, walking up slopes, and walking down slopes. Pavement up and pavement down were removed as they only represent transient transitions on and off curbs spanning a single step. 
We included only the tri-axial accelerometer and gyroscope from the IMU sensors excluding the magnetometer due to its susceptibility to magnetic interference. As the dataset expressed the IMU data in the global coordinate frame, we reoriented the coordinate system to each sensor's local coordinate system so that changes in heading would not affect model performance. This transformation was implemented using custom Python code, available at \href{https://github.com/jillemmerzaal/heading-direction}{https://github.com/jillemmerzaal/heading-direction}.

\subsubsection{Sensor configurations}
We used 2 IMU sensor configurations. First, we used a 7 sensor ``Lower Limb'' configuration with sensors on the pelvis, upper legs, lower legs, and feet (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensor_config}a). Secondly we mirrored the ``Luo'' configuration containing 6 sensors placed on the right forearm, pelvis, upper and lower legs (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensor_config}b). The ``Luo'' configuration was selected to allow direct comparison with previous studies ~\cite{Shah_2022}.

The pressure insoles contain 8 sensors: arch, hallux, heel L, heel R, met 1, met 3, met 5, toes. We only included the raw pressure data from the insoles. 
As such, for both ML and DL approaches we tested four different models: (1) IMU sensors in Lower Limb configuration,  (2) IMU sensors in Luo configuration, (3) foot pressure insoles alone, and (4) combined IMU sensors in Lower Limb configuration and foot pressure insoles. 

\subsubsection{Data Segmentation}
We implemented two distinct strategies for data segmentation.  The first strategy, gait segmentation, relied on gait event detection captured with the pressure insole data supplied in the dataset: instances when the feet came into contact with the ground were tagged~\cite{losing_2022}. From these annotations, we segmented the gait cycles (n=29,988) and normalized them to 100 data points to represent 100\% of a gait cycle. This is consistent with standard biomechanical practices. %Thereafter, we extracted gait features from the normalized gait trials. 
The second strategy, sliding window segmentation, extracted data using a a sliding window algorithm with a window size of 2 seconds and a step size of 1 second. %(n=30,444). 
Sliding window segmentation is more consistent with data-driven approaches, and could be used when step detection is not available or is considered too variable. Moreover, models do not depend on a step detection algorithm or gait style. The window and step sizes were chosen to maintain a nearly similar number of windows and gait cycles from the gait segmentation strategy, ensuring that any differences in performance could not be attributed to variations in input segment length. Approximately 30,000 segments were obtained for each segmentation method. 

\subsection{Machine Learning Approach}
\subsubsection{Feature Engineering and Reduction}
For the machine learning approach, feature engineering was performed prior to model development using the Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation) packages Pandas \cite{reback2020pandas}, Numpy \cite{harris2020array} and Scikit-learn \cite{scikit-learn}.

% Prior to feature calculation, additional features related to orientation and elevation changes were introduced. The elevation difference between consecutive data points was approximated by processing the pelvis acceleration signal on the z-axis. This signal was integrated over time (60 Hz sampling frequency) to calculate velocity and then integrated again to estimate position. The difference in position between consecutive data points was used to determine elevation changes.

% For orientation, gyroscope data from the pelvis sensor along all three axes (x, y, z) were used. These data provided rotational velocities, which were integrated to compute orientation angles. To reduce noise, a second-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 5 Hz was applied before integration. The use of IMUs mounted on the pelvis for tracking three-dimensional orientation angles has been validated in prior studies \cite{proceedings2020049010}.

Statistical and frequency based features were calculated for each gait cycle and sliding window. The statistical features include minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, interquartile range, mean absolute deviation, area under the curve, skewness, kurtosis, and entropy using a histogram-based method with 10 bins. Frequency-based features are the first five discrete Fourier transform coefficients and the weighted mean frequency. This feature set is similar to those available in packages such as the Time Series Feature Extraction based on Scalable Hypothesis tests (TSFRESH)~\cite{tsfresh_2018} and, by the nature of the feature engineering, our approach inscribes itself into the realm of Statistically Enhanced Learning~\cite{felice2025boosting}. 
Feature reduction was performed using filter methods to optimize the dataset (c.f.~\cite{saeys_2007}). For features with a correlation threshold of 0.8 or higher, only one feature (randomly selected) from each correlated set was retained. Features with no variation across the dataset and those with a low variation coefficient (less than 0.1) were removed. This approach to feature extraction follows common good practices and should help define a reproducible method.

\subsubsection{Model Training and Evaluation}
The preprocessed and reduced dataset was split into training (80\%) and test (20\%) sets using a subject-wise splitting approach to ensure that all data from a single participant were exclusively in either the training or the test set \cite{Saeb2017}. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm \cite{chen2016xgboost} was selected as the ML model for classification due to its robustness and high performance in structured data classification tasks. We implemented a Stratified Group k-Fold cross-validation approach to tune the hyperparameters. Ensuring that the train and validation folds did not contain data from the same participant, thus, reducing the risk of data leakage and improving the model's generalizability~\cite{Shah_2022}. Hyperparameters such as learning rate, maximum depth, and the number of estimators were optimized using a grid search.
We assessed model stability by repeating the training and evaluation steps 10 times with different random seeds for the data split. Model performance was evaluated using the mean and standard deviation value of several metrics (accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, and specificity) across the 10 repeated trials. Sensitivity and specificity were included to align with clinical evaluation standards.
% Confusion matrices were generated to assess classification performance across different surface types, providing insights into misclassification rates. The XGBoost model demonstrated high performance in distinguishing between various surfaces, with sensitivity and specificity indicating its applicability in clinical and real-world scenarios.


\subsection{Deep Learning approach}
We developed a DL pipeline using common methods of good practice for a one-dimensional CNN. This architecture is particularly well-suited for this task, designed to effectively capture local temporal patterns in sequential sensor data \cite{ref1dcnn}. We employed the same subject-wise data split as the ML approach to ensure uniformity. Feature columns were standardized using a StandardScaler, and categorical walking labels were encoded using a LabelEncoder and one-hot encoding. A CNN architecture was defined and tuned using Keras Tuner’s random search strategy, optimizing hyperparameters such as the number of filters, kernel size, dense layer units, dropout rate, and learning rate. The model was trained using a categorical cross-entropy loss function and Adam optimizer, with class weights applied to address label imbalance. Early stopping was employed to prevent overfitting. %We used the same approach as in the ML pipeline to assess model stability.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:Results}

Tables \ref{tab:ml_gait_cycle_seg} and \ref{tab:dl_gait_cycle_seg} summarize the performance of the ML and DL models for different sensor configurations and segmentation strategies, respectively. Across both approaches, models using IMU lower limb data consistently achieved the highest accuracy and F1-scores compared to those using pressure insoles alone. Combining IMU and pressure insole data produced marginal improvements over IMU alone. For the ML models, gait cycle segmentation slightly improved performance relative to the sliding window approach, with the highest F1-score of 0.87 and accuracy of 0.86 observed for the IMU lower limb configuration combined with pressure insoles. In contrast, DL models demonstrated strong and consistent performance across segmentation types, with the combined IMU and pressure insole configuration using sliding window segmentation achieving the best overall results (accuracy = 0.90, F1 = 0.90).

The ML model showed the greatest confusion with sloped walking and flat walking, where sloped down walking was predicted to be level walking at least 41\% of the time (Figure~\ref{fig:cm}a); the DL model had the greatest difficulty predicting level walking, which was predicted to be sloped up walking 29\% of the time (Figure~\ref{fig:cm}b).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary}
This study investigated the performance of different sensor configurations and segmentation strategies in classifying walking conditions using ML and DL models. Across sensor configurations, models using IMU data consistently outperformed those using pressure insoles alone, reaffirming the superior discriminative capability of IMU sensors for walking condition classification. Combining IMU with pressure insole data produced results comparable to IMU-only configurations and did not consistently improve performance. There were no distinct differences between the two segmentation methods implemented. The data-driven sliding window approach may thus be preferable, as it does not rely on gait event detection algorithms.


\subsection{Effect of sensor input}
Our findings partially support the first hypothesis, confirming that IMU sensors provide more discriminative information than pressure insoles across both modeling approaches, at least for the technology (resistive, 8 fields), particular model, and raw pressure data used. The highest F1-scores for IMU-based models reached 0.87 in the ML approach and 0.90 in the DL approach. In contrast, models relying solely on pressure insole data demonstrated substantially lower performance, with peak F1-scores of 0.61 for ML and 0.52 for DL. Models combining both sensor modalities showed modest gains of approximately 1-percentage point increase in accuracy for all configurations except ML model using a sliding window, compared to IMU only models. This indicates that, although pressure insoles capture complementary aspects of foot-ground interaction, their integration with IMU data provides only limited additional benefit. Moreover, all classification accuracies across models, except for the pressure model, are all within 1 standard deviation from each other. These results indicate that sensor configuration can be determined based on the constraints of the experimental protocol while retaining similar model performance. 

\subsection{Effect of trail segmentation method}
Our second hypothesis was conditionally supported. There were no major differences between the two segmentation methods (gait cycle segmentation versus sliding window). Close inspection reveals a slight trend suggesting that ML models perform better with gait segmentation (F1: 0.87 $\pm$ 0.04) than with sliding window (F1: 0.84 $\pm$ 0.04), while DL models tend to benefit more from sliding window (F1: 0.90 $\pm$ 0.03) than from gait cycle segmentation (F1: 0.88 $\pm$ 0.03). It is important to highlight that these F1-scores are all within 1 standard deviation from each other. Thereby, we cannot draw any firm conclusions about the superiority of either method from these results. Still, that DL models obtain higher classification accuracies with the sliding window approach greatly facilitate data processing and can make DL models more robust, as no step detection algorithm needs to be implemented to achieve strong performance. 

\subsection{Comparison to previous work}
Compared to previous work, our results show an improvement in the classification of outdoor walking conditions on several fronts. Our models show similar or improved results compared to previous work~\cite{Chen2019, Hu_2021, Shah_2022} with the benefit of including a sampling of more clinically relevant surfaces and recorded in a context that more closely reflects real life walking conditions (improved ecological validity). Comparatively, Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022} found an excellent F1-score of 0.96 using a CNN model with only the right shank sensor; however, to achieve these excellent performances, they needed participant calibration data during model training. Without relying on calibration data for model training, their F1-score  was reduced to 0.78. %(Note: The aim of Shah et al.'s paper was to demonstrate the effect of participant calibration and did not seek to develop models with the highest performance \cite{Shah_2022}). 
As it is not always feasible to obtain surface calibration per participant, it is advantageous in terms of deployment capabilities to develop models that do not  require this additional processing step. In our data processing, we used subject-wise stratification, thereby ensuring that no data of the participants were in both the training- and test-set while achieving more favorable F1-scores compared to Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022}. 

\subsection{Surface specific performance}

Based on the highest performing model (ML: gait segmented pressure insoles + IMUs; DL: sliding window segmentation pressure insoles + IMU) we found that the ML model is excellent at classifying stairs negotiation. Classifying down slope walking, however, appears to be challenging (ML accuracy: 59\%, with 41\% of slope down trials incorrectly identified as walking) (Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}a). This may be explained by the sloped surfaces used herein. Contrary to most surface classification papers, this data-set contains two slope types, one that is long ($\pm$ 50m) with a gradient of 6\% and another that is short (3m) with a gradient of 15\%. Comparatively, the Luo et al. data-set has a slope of 8.33\%~\cite{Luo_2020} featured in ~\cite{Shah_2022, chauhan2025classifying, yildiz2023towards, Hu_2021} or a slope of 36\% featured in Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2019}. Combining two slope types might have confused the ML model resulting in the gradual slope being identified as level walking. A slope with a gradient of 6 \% is also quite shallow (less than the maximum of 8.33\% requirement for Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) compliant ramps) and was presumably not overly challenging, nor requiring major biomechanical adaptations for the population studied. The DL model, on the contrary, is better able to predict the slopes, regardless of grade differences, but was less able to distinguish level and stairs down walking (0.71\% and 0.74\% respectively) (Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}b). The model incorrectly identified 29\% of walking trials as slope up and 26\% of stairs down trials as slope up. These results are on the lower end compared to previous work that showed accuracies above 90\% on differentiating between stairs and level walking surfaces~\cite{Chen2019}. The ecological validity of data from this data-set, including the different slopes and the changes in direction during level walking make our results more generalizable in classifying surfaces in real life conditions. 

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
There are four main limitations related to features, model architectures, populations sampled, and sensor set-up that warrant discussion and can be used to guide future work. 

First, in the ML model, we relied on generic (statistical- and frequency-based) features instead of domain specific-features. This may have hindered the model's ability to fully leverage the available gait information. This was done intentionally as we sought a fair comparison with the DL model. Incorporating (biomechanical) domain-specific features may provide the models with more discriminative information and improve their predictive performance. Also, we did not leverage advanced feature selection techniques (e.g. Salp Swarm Algorithm~\cite{mirjalili2017salp}) or feature extraction techniques that incorporate sensor fusion (e.g. Time Series Fusion (TSFuse)~\cite{de2024tsfuse}). The Salp Swarm Algorithm, as used in Chauhan et al.~\cite{chauhan2025classifying}, effectively reduces feature dimensionality while maintaining high accuracy. Though it is unclear whether Chauchan et al.~\cite{chauhan2025classifying} used a subject-wise split, making direct comparison with our results difficult. Moreover, we considered all our input data as univariate,  assuming no interaction between the signals. As we used a multiple sensor set-up, we might have missed information related to  the interactions of the different sensors. De Brabandere et al.~\cite{de2024tsfuse} developed an automated feature construction system (TSFuse) that fuses data from multivariate time series both within and between sensors, thereby creating new, and possibly relevant, time series~\cite{de2024tsfuse}. Future work could investigate the effect of these advanced and automated feature extraction tools to further refine model performance. 

Second, we only focused on two model architectures: a 1D CNN for the DL approach and XGBoost for the ML approach. The 1D CNN was chosen for its effectiveness in capturing local temporal patterns in sequential sensor data while maintaining a relatively low computational cost compared to more complex architectures like Long Short-Term Memory Units. Its structure is well-suited for processing multivariate time series such as IMU and pressure insole data, making it an efficient and interpretable choice for our classification task. XGBoost was selected for its strong performance with structured feature-based data, robustness to overfitting, and efficiency in handling high-dimensional input. Although we limited our exploration to these two well-established architectures to maintain a focused and interpretable evaluation, future work may benefit from exploring alternative or more advanced models tailored to specific sensor modalities.

Third, expanding surface classification studies to different populations remains a very important area for exploration. At this moment, studies have only used asymptomatic, uninjured subjects. While it is important to investigate the capabilities of classifying outdoor walking surfaces in unconstrained individuals, it will limit the generalizability of the models. It is unclear if models would misclassify surfaces due to an underlying gait pathology or when using walking aids. Therefore, future work needs to include participants with musculoskeletal and/or neurological injury for these models to be applicable for clinical biomechanics in free living environments.  

Lastly, our models depend on a complex sensor set-up. To achieve the best prediction F1-scores, we needed six or seven IMU sensors combined with pressure insole data. Future work should investigate methods to reduce the number of sensors, allowing for improved real-world applicability and feasibility.
