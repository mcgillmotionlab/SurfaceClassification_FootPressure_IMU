\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Walking in the natural and built outdoor environment is a common task for a majority of humans. Often, surface topologies, such as stairs, ramps, or irregular surfaces, may be encountered which may pose challenges to ageing populations or those with gait or mobility issues. In work conducted in Norway, Bergland et al.~\cite{Oloughlin_f1994} reported that 59\% of falls in older adults occurred outdoors (n = 431, ages 67–97), while in Canada (Montreal) fall incidence rates between indoor and outdoor environments were 21.2 and 20.4 per 1000 person-months, respectively (n = 556 age $>$ 65 years)~\cite{Oloughlin_f1994}. Although, winter conditions may affect indoor/outdoor ratios, surface types may also be relevant. For example, Li et al.~\cite{li_2006} found that outdoor falls were mainly (47.6\%) precipitated by uneven surfaces for middle-age and older adults living in a temperate climate. 

Previous studies have established that these surfaces illicit biomechanical gait adaptations, compared to flat-surface walking. As such,

Machine learning algorithms have shown potential in automatically identifying outdoor surfaces from both image and sensor data. Using image data collected from chest-mounted smartphones, Laschowski et al.~\cite{Laschowski_2022} identified surfaces such as stairs up and down, level ground,  and surface transitions via convolutional neural networks; however, they did not include relevant outdoor surface features such as irregular or sloped surfaces. Follow-up work by Whipps et al. (CITATION XXX) integrated a larger number of surfaces and demonstrated that additional LiDar sensor data, available in many latest-generation smartphones, improved prediction accuracy from 82 to 92\% for image only models vs models trained on both image and Lidar data. The most popular approach to date, however, has been the development of predictive algorithms from inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors worn on body segments. Of these, Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022} were able to classify surfaces () with F1 scores between 0.78 and 0.96 based on a public data set from Luo et al.~\cite{Luo_2020}.

%Also cite NACOB data descriptor

Although these approaches are promising, it is not feasible to implement these data collection modalities, due to compliance issues. It remains unknown if machine learning algorithms based on less obtrusive sensors could also provide strong surface prediction capabilities.  

Thus, the primary aim of this study is to (1) develop a machine learning model for the identification of walking terrain based on foot pressure sensor data. The secondary aims are to (2) assess whether the addition of IMU sensor data improves model performance and if (3) gait cycle segmentation improves model performance. We hypothesize that:  (1) there will be sufficient information in the foot pressure sensor data to identify walking surfaces; (2) addition of IMU data and (3) use of gait cycle segmentation pre-processing will improve model performance.  


\section{Methods}
\label{sec:Methods}

\subsection{Data acquisition}

Data set from~\cite{losing_2022} was used in this study, it is a motion capture experiment that recorded data using Xsens IMUs (XSENS REF) and pressure insoles. (INSOLES REF) The dataset includes 3D kinematic data and ground reaction forces, collected as participants walked on various surface configurations, including straight and curvy passages, slopes, stairs, and pavements. the data was annotated by walk mode (walk, stairs\_up, stairs\_down, slope\_up, slope\_down, pavement\_up, pavement\_
down) and they were pre-aligned and synchronized, facilitating the merging of Xsens and pressure insole data. Each session in the dataset reflects different surface conditions and walk mode. 

\subsection{Feature Engineering and Reduction}
Feature engineering was performed prior to model development using the Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation) packages Pandas(REF), Numpy and Scikit-learn. (REF) Statistical features were calculated for each trial on the different surfaces. These features included mean, maximum (max), minimum (min), interquartile range (iqr), mean absolute deviation (mad), standard deviation (std), and area under the curve (auc).
Feature reduction was performed using filter methods to optimize the dataset. Features with a correlation threshold of 0.8 or higher were discarded, retaining only the first feature of each correlated set. Features with no variation across the dataset and those with a low variation coefficient (less than 0.1) were also removed.

\subsection{Model Training and Evaluation}
The preprocessed and reduced dataset was split into training (80\%) and test (20\%) sets using a subject-wise splitting approach to ensure that all data from a single participant were exclusively in either the training or the test set. (REF) The XGBoost algorithm (REF) was selected as the machine learning model for surface classification due to its robustness and high performance in structured data classification tasks. A grid search was conducted for hyperparameter tuning, optimizing parameters such as learning rate, maximum depth, and the number of estimators. A 5-fold cross-validation approach was employed to ensure the model's generalizability and to prevent overfitting. The model’s performance was evaluated using accuracy as the primary metric.
