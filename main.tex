\abstract{(1) Background: Navigating surfaces during walking can alter gait patterns. This study aims to develop tools for automatic walking condition classification using inertial measurement unit (IMU) and foot pressure sensors. We compared sensor modalities (IMUs \textcolor{revision}{on lower-limbs, IMUs on feet}, pressure insoles, and IMUs on feet combined with pressure insoles) and evaluated whether gait cycle segmentation, \textcolor{revision}{compared to a sliding window}, improves performance. 
(2) Methods: Twenty participants performed flat, stairs up, stairs down, slope up, and slope down walking trials \textcolor{revision}{while fit with} IMUs and pressure insoles. (3) Results: \textcolor{revision}{Overall, a DL model using lower-limb IMUs, processed with a sliding window, performed best (F1=0.91). (4) Conclusions: 
IMUs provide the most discriminative features for automatic walking condition classification. Combining sensor modalities may be helpful for some model architectures. DL models perform well without gait segmentation making them independent of gait event identification algorithms.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Walking in the natural and built outdoor environment is a common task for most individuals that requires navigating various surface topologies such as stairs, ramps, or irregular surfaces.  
From previous work, we know that surface topology alters biomechanical gait patterns~\cite{ippersiel_impact_2022, Dixon_2018, dixon_late-cueing_2018, vieira_gait_2017, menz_acceleration_2003, emmerzaal_non-linear_2025}. %Recent work has shown that even in healthy, young adults, outdoor walking surfaces elicit biomechanical adaptations~\cite{emmerzaal_non-linear_2025, ippersiel_impact_2022}.
These adaptations could be challenging for aging populations and individuals with gait or mobility issues. In fact, uneven surfaces have been identified as a major factor in outdoor falls among middle-aged and older adults~\cite{li_2006}. %Moreover, Bergland et al.~\cite{Bergland_1998} reported that 59\% of falls in older adults in Norway occurred outdoors, while in Canada fall incidence rates between indoor and outdoor environments were nearly equal at 20-21\%~\cite{Oloughlin_f1994}.
As such, the ability to incorporate outdoor environments in biomechanical analysis would allow us to fully capture the demands of walking. Accordingly, valid and easy-to-use algorithms to classify walking conditions for remote monitoring of real-life behavior are required.

\textcolor{revision}{Finally}, the effect of data segmentation on model performance is largely unknown. Previous work used either segmentation on gait cycles~\cite{Shah_2022} or no segmentation at all~\cite{chauhan2025classifying}. As walking condition can influence biomechanics and thus the signal representation within different steps, it is crucial to explore whether data segmentation methods matter. There are two common methods, first, the traditional biomechanical gait cycle segmentation, and second, a more data-driven sliding window approach. Segmentation based on gait cycles requires domain knowledge and identification of gait events, whereas fixed window segmentation requires no domain knowledge and would make resulting models independent of gait event identification algorithms. 

Therefore, the goal of this study is to develop and validate tools for automatic walking condition classification using IMU sensor and pressure \textcolor{revision}{insole} data. By addressing these research aims, this study contributes to enhancing the ecological validity of ML and DL models in classifying surface topology, thereby aiding biomechanical analysis to occur unconstrained during outdoor walking.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:Methods}

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Segmentation}
\subsubsection{Data Acquisition}
We leveraged a pre-existing dataset by Losing \& Hasenj{\"a}ger (2022)~\cite{losing_2022} in this study. Briefly, the dataset represents a motion capture experiment that recorded data using an Xsens motion capture suit consisting of 17 IMU sensors \cite{xsens} and IEE ActiSense Smart Footwear Sensor pressure insoles \cite{foot_pressure} as participants walked across real-world environments. The data were collected from 20 participants (5 females, ages 18-69 years) walking 3 courses of approximately 500m (Course A, B, C) where they encountered various walking conditions. Data were provided at the downsampled rate of 60 Hz. For additional details, see the original publication (c.f.~\cite{losing_2022}).

\subsection{Data Preparation}
For this study, we reduced the dataset from seven to five classes that were annotated in the database: \textcolor{revision}{flat walking}, walking up stairs, walking down stairs, walking up slopes, and walking down slopes. Pavement up and pavement down were removed as they only represent transient transitions on and off curbs spanning a single step. 
We included only the tri-axial accelerometer and gyroscope \textcolor{revision}{signals} from the IMU sensors excluding the magnetometer due to its susceptibility to magnetic interference. As the dataset expressed the IMU data in the global coordinate frame, we reoriented the coordinate system to each \textcolor{revision}{segment's} local coordinate system \textcolor{revision}{following the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) recommendations~\cite{wu2002isb}} so that changes in heading would not affect model performance. This transformation was implemented using custom Python code, available at \href{https://github.com/jillemmerzaal/heading-direction}{https://github.com/jillemmerzaal/heading-direction}.

\subsubsection{Sensor configurations}
We used 2 IMU sensor configurations. First, we used a 7 sensor ``Lower Limb'' configuration with sensors on the pelvis, upper legs, lower legs, and feet (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensor_config}). \textcolor{revision}{This lower limb model has performed well with predicting surface topology in previous work (c.f.~\cite{chauhan2025classifying}) and will be referred to as the established model, herein. The second configuration focused on IMU sensors mounted on the feet only to allow for a direct comparison with the third configuration which uses the pressure insoles.}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=6.0cm]{figures/lower limb config.png}
\caption{Schematic representation of the IMU sensor set-ups used in this study. Seven-sensor ``Lower Limb Configuration'' (Pelvis, Upper legs, Lower legs, and Feet) includes all sensors circled in teal.}

The pressure insoles contain 8 sensors: arch, hallux, heel L, heel R, met 1, met 3, met 5, toes. We only included the  pressure data \textcolor{revision}{normalized to each participant's body mass} from the insoles. 
As such, for both ML and DL approaches, we tested four different models: (1) IMU sensors in Lower Limb configuration (IMU lower-limbs), (2) IMU sensors \textcolor{revision}{on the feet (IMU feet)}, (3) foot pressure insoles alone (Pressure feet), and (4) combined modalities (IMU \textcolor{revision}{feet + Pressure feet)}. 

\subsubsection{Data Segmentation}
We implemented two distinct strategies for data segmentation.  The first strategy, gait segmentation, relied on gait event detection captured with the pressure insole data supplied in the dataset: instances when the feet came into contact with the ground were tagged~\cite{losing_2022}. From these annotations, we segmented the gait cycles and normalized them to 101 data points to represent 100\% of a gait cycle. This is consistent with standard biomechanical practices \textcolor{revision}{(Figure~\ref{fig:joint_angles})}. %Thereafter, we extracted gait features from the normalized gait trials. 
The second strategy, sliding window segmentation, extracted data using a sliding window algorithm with a window size of 2 seconds and a step size of 1 second. %(n=30,444). 
Sliding window segmentation is more consistent with data-driven approaches, and could be used when step detection is not available or is considered too variable. Moreover, models do not depend on a step detection algorithm or gait style. The window and step sizes were chosen to maintain a nearly similar number of windows and gait cycles from the gait segmentation strategy, ensuring that any differences in performance could not be attributed to variations in input segment length. Approximately 30,000 segments were obtained for each segmentation method \textcolor{revision}{which confirms that our experimental window and step size aligns with the average duration of a gait cycle.}

\subsection{Machine Learning Approach}
\subsubsection{Feature Engineering and Reduction}
For the machine learning approach, feature engineering was performed prior to model development using the Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation) packages Pandas \cite{reback2020pandas}, Numpy \cite{harris2020array} and Scikit-learn \cite{scikit-learn}.

% Prior to feature calculation, additional features related to orientation and elevation changes were introduced. The elevation difference between consecutive data points was approximated by processing the pelvis acceleration signal on the z-axis. This signal was integrated over time (60 Hz sampling frequency) to calculate velocity and then integrated again to estimate position. The difference in position between consecutive data points was used to determine elevation changes.

% For orientation, gyroscope data from the pelvis sensor along all three axes (x, y, z) were used. These data provided rotational velocities, which were integrated to compute orientation angles. To reduce noise, a second-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 5 Hz was applied before integration. The use of IMUs mounted on the pelvis for tracking three-dimensional orientation angles has been validated in prior studies \cite{proceedings2020049010}.

Statistical and frequency based features were calculated for each gait cycle and sliding window. The statistical features include minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, interquartile range, mean absolute deviation, area under the curve, skewness, kurtosis, and entropy using a histogram-based method with 10 bins. Frequency-based features are the first five discrete Fourier transform coefficients and the weighted mean frequency. This feature set is similar to those available in packages such as the Time Series Feature Extraction based on Scalable Hypothesis tests (TSFRESH)~\cite{tsfresh_2018} and, by the nature of the feature engineering, our approach inscribes itself into the realm of Statistically Enhanced Learning~\cite{felice2025boosting}. 
Feature reduction was performed using filter methods to optimize the dataset (c.f.~\cite{saeys_2007}). For features with a correlation threshold of 0.8 or higher, only one feature (randomly selected) from each correlated set was retained. Features with no variation across the dataset and those with a low variation coefficient (less than 0.1) were removed. This approach to feature extraction follows common good practices and should help define a reproducible method.

\subsubsection{Model Training and Evaluation}
The preprocessed and reduced dataset was split into training (80\%) and test (20\%) sets using a subject-wise splitting approach to ensure that all data from a single participant were exclusively in either the training or the test set \cite{Saeb2017}. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm \cite{chen2016xgboost} was selected as the ML model for classification due to its robustness and high performance in structured data classification tasks. We implemented a Stratified Group k-Fold cross-validation approach to tune the hyperparameters. Ensuring that the train and validation folds did not contain data from the same participant, thus, reducing the risk of data leakage and improving the model's generalizability~\cite{Shah_2022}. Hyperparameters such as learning rate, maximum depth, and the number of estimators were optimized using a grid search.
We assessed model stability by repeating the training and evaluation steps 10 times with different random seeds for the data split. Model performance was evaluated using the mean and standard deviation value of several metrics (accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, and specificity) across the 10 repeated trials. Sensitivity and specificity were included to align with clinical evaluation standards.
% Confusion matrices were generated to assess classification performance across different surface types, providing insights into misclassification rates. The XGBoost model demonstrated high performance in distinguishing between various surfaces, with sensitivity and specificity indicating its applicability in clinical and real-world scenarios.


\subsection{Deep Learning approach}
\textcolor{revision}{We developed an advanced deep learning pipeline using the same subject-wise data splitting strategy as mentioned earlier. All input features were standardized using a z-score transformation. The final architecture integrates multiple temporal-processing components designed to capture different scales of gait dynamics (Fig.~\ref{fig:model_architecture}). Initial Residual Conv1D blocks extract robust local temporal–spatial features while improving gradient flow and reducing overfitting. These are followed by a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) module with dilated convolutions to capture stride-level and multi-stride dependencies. A Bidirectional LSTM layer then models longer-range sequential structure inherent in cyclic gait patterns. Finally, a Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHA) mechanism adaptively weights the most informative time steps, enhancing interpretability by revealing which temporal regions contribute most strongly to classification. To optimize performance, we applied Keras Tuner’s random search to tune key hyperparameters, including the number of convolutional filters, convolutional kernel sizes, LSTM units, the number of attention heads, dense-layer width, dropout rate, and learning rate. The model was trained with categorical cross-entropy and the Adam optimizer, using class weights to address label imbalance and early stopping to prevent overfitting. This type of hybrid CNN–TCN–LSTM–attention architecture is well-supported in the gait literature, as combining convolutional, recurrent, and attention mechanisms has been shown to more effectively model both fine-grained and long-range temporal dependencies in human gait \cite{burges2024gait, 10862239, LV2025199}.}

\textcolor{revision}{
\subsection{Model comparison}
To test our hypotheses for the ML and DL models, we performed eight separate paired t-tests of the F1-scores ($\alpha = 0.05$). For our first hypothesis, we tested the difference in model performance between the IMU feet and Pressure feet models. To test whether adding pressure insoles to IMUs enhances model performance, we tested the difference between the IMU feet and the combined IMU feet + Pressure feet model. 
For our second hypothesis, we tested the difference between segmentation approaches (sliding window vs gait segmentation) for the combined IMU feet + Pressure feet model. As we also wanted to know if our minimally intrusive model performed better than a state-of-the-art full IMU sensor model (established model), we tested the difference between the combined IMU feet + Pressure feet model and the IMU lower-limbs model.}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary}

This study investigated the performance of different sensor configurations and segmentation strategies in classifying walking conditions using ML and DL models. Across sensor configurations, models using IMU data consistently outperformed those using pressure insoles alone, reaffirming the superior discriminative capability of IMU sensors for walking condition classification. Combining IMUs \textcolor{revision}{on the foot} with pressure insole data produced results comparable to \textcolor{revision}{IMU foot}-only configurations for DL \textcolor{revision}{models}; however, \textcolor{revision}{it significantly improved surface classification for ML models}. Machine learning significantly benefited from gait segmentation  over sliding window segmentation. Conversely, there were no distinct differences between the two segmentation methods implemented \textcolor{revision}{for deep learning. The data-driven sliding window approach may thus be preferable \textcolor{revision}{for deep learning}, as it does not rely on gait event detection algorithms. } 

\subsection{Effect of sensor input}
Our findings partially support the first hypothesis, confirming that IMU sensors provide more discriminative information than pressure insoles across both modeling approaches, at least for the technology (resistive, 8 fields), particular model, and \textcolor{revision}{normalized} pressure data used. The highest F1-scores \textcolor{revision}{were found for the established IMU lower-limbs} models, reaching F1=0.87 in the ML approach and \textcolor{revision}{F1=0.91} in the DL approach. In contrast, models relying solely on pressure insole data demonstrated substantially lower performance, with peak F1-scores of \textcolor{revision}{0.66} for ML and \textcolor{revision}{0.71} for DL. Machine learning models combining both sensor modalities showed gains of approximately \textcolor{revision}{9-percentage} points in performance \textcolor{revision}{compared to the IMU feet model using gait segmentation, and approximately 12-percentage point increase for sliding window segmentation}. \textcolor{revision}{In contrast, DL models only showed minimal gains of 2-percentage points for sliding window and no gains with gait segmentation.} This indicates that, although pressure insoles capture complementary aspects of foot-ground interaction, their integration with IMU data only provides benefit \textcolor{revision}{using machine learning. Finally, the combined model (IMU feet + Pressure feet) was not able to achieve comparable performance to the established IMU lower-limbs model, reaffirming that additional information from sensors on the lower-limbs are important in classifying surfaces during walking.}

% Moreover, all classification accuracies across models, except for the pressure model, are all within 1 standard deviation from each other. These results indicate that sensor configuration can be determined based on the constraints of the experimental protocol while retaining similar model performance. 

\subsection{Effect of trial segmentation method}
Our second hypothesis was supported \textcolor{revision}{for ML, but not for DL}. There were \textcolor{revision}{significant} differences between the two segmentation methods \textcolor{revision}{for ML, where gait cycle segmentation ($\overline{F1}=0.80$) significantly outperformed the sliding window segmentation ($\overline{F1}=0.77$) using the combined IMU feet + Pressure feet model. For DL,} close inspection reveals a slight trend suggesting that DL tend to benefit more from sliding window (F1= 0.83 $\pm$ 0.04) than from gait cycle segmentation (F1= 0.80 $\pm$ 0.05). \textcolor{revision}{Although, we did not find a significant difference for the DL model, that they obtain similar} classification accuracies with the sliding window approach greatly facilitate data processing and can make DL models more robust, as no step detection algorithm needs to be implemented to achieve strong performance.

% It is important to highlight that these F1-scores are all within 1 standard deviation from each other. Thereby, we cannot draw any firm conclusions about the superiority of either method from these results. Still, that DL models obtain higher classification accuracies with the sliding window approach greatly facilitate data processing and can make DL models more robust, as no step detection algorithm needs to be implemented to achieve strong performance. 

\subsection{Comparison to previous work}
Compared to previous work, our results show an improvement in the classification of outdoor walking conditions on several fronts.  As it is not always feasible to obtain surface calibration per participant, it is advantageous in terms of deployment capabilities to develop models that do not require this additional processing step. In our data processing, we used subject-wise stratification, thereby ensuring that no data of the participants were in both the training- and test-set. \textcolor{revision}{Of the 17 studies found that classify surface topology, only 4 use a subject-wise or leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) train test split~\cite{Shah_2022, ng2022machine, kobayashi2019estimation, worsey2021automatic} (Table~\ref{tab:prev-models}). Of these, Ng et al.~\cite{ng2022machine} found an area under the curve of 0.80 using an SVM model with a single sensor on the right ankle; however, they trained only a binary classifier capable of predicting irregular vs. regular surfaces. More comparable to our work are the studies of Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022} and Kobayashi et al.~\cite{kobayashi2019estimation}. Kobayashi et al.~\cite{kobayashi2019estimation} used a smartphone (location unspecified) to capture six different surfaces; however, they were only able to achieve an accuracy of 44.9\% using a LOSO split. In order for their model to perform well, subject data needed to be captured and used in training and testing (accuracy increases to 83.5\%). Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022} achieved a classification F1-score of 0.78 using a subject-wise split classifying 9 outdoor surfaces with either the IMU lower-limbs model or a model using only an IMU on the right shank. Our combined model achieved slightly more favorable F1-scores (DL sliding window, 0.83). Moreover, the outdoor walking in this work is unconstrained; meaning, participants walked a continuous course encountering different surfaces without stopping.}
\textcolor{revision}{As such, our minimally intrusive IMU feet + Pressure feet sensor set-up} show improved results compared to previous work with the benefit of including a sampling of more clinically relevant surfaces and recorded in a context that more closely reflects real life walking conditions (improved ecological validity).

% Comparatively, Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022} found an excellent F1-score of 0.96 using a CNN model with only the right shank sensor; however, to achieve these excellent performances, they needed participant calibration data during model training. Without relying on calibration data for model training, their F1-score  was reduced to 0.78. %(Note: The aim of Shah et al.'s paper was to demonstrate the effect of participant calibration and did not seek to develop models with the highest performance \cite{Shah_2022}). 
% As it is not always feasible to obtain surface calibration per participant, it is advantageous in terms of deployment capabilities to develop models that do not require this additional processing step. In our data processing, we used subject-wise stratification, thereby ensuring that no data of the participants were in both the training- and test-set while achieving more favorable F1-scores compared to Shah et al.~\cite{Shah_2022}. 

\subsection{Surface specific performance}

%Based on the highest performing model (ML: gait segmented pressure insoles + IMUs foot; DL: sliding window segmentation pressure insoles + IMUs foot) 
We found that the best ML model is excellent at classifying stair negotiation (accuracy of 0.93 and 0.91 for stairs down and stairs up, respectively), but struggles with slopes (accuracy of 0.67 and 0.35 for slope down and slope up, respectively) (Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}a). Slope down and slope up were confused with flat walking 33\% and 64\% of the time, respectively. This may be explained by the sloped surfaces used herein. Contrary to most surface classification papers, this dataset contains two slope types, one that is long (\textcolor{revision}{50-70}m) with a gradient of 6\% and another that is short (3m) with a gradient of 15\%. 
\textcolor{revision}{Moreover, across the dataset  there are fewer instances of the steep, compared to shallow, slope (60 vs 300, respectively).} Comparatively, the Luo et al.~\cite{Luo_2020} dataset has a slope of 8.33\% featured in ~\cite{Shah_2022, chauhan2025classifying, yildiz2023towards, Hu_2021} \textcolor{revision}{while Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2019}
studied} a slope of 36\%. 
%Combining two slope types might have confused the ML model resulting in the gradual slope being identified as level walking.
A slope with a gradient of 6\% is also quite shallow (less than the maximum of 8.33\% requirement for Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) compliant ramps) and was presumably not overly challenging, nor requiring major biomechanical adaptations for the population studied, \textcolor{revision}{making it difficult for the model to distinguish from flat walking.} The DL model, on the contrary, is better able to predict the slopes (accuracy of 0.71 and 0.63 for slope down and slope up, respectively), regardless of grade differences, while maintaining strong performance on the stairs (accuracy of 1.00 and 0.99 for stairs down and stairs up, respectively) (Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}b).
%but was less able to distinguish level and stairs down walking (0.71\% and 0.74\% respectively) . 
The model incorrectly identified 29\% of walking trials as slope up and 37\% of walking trials as slope down. 
%These results are on the lower end compared to previous work that showed accuracies above 90\% on differentiating between stairs and flat walking surfaces~\cite{Chen2019}. 
The ecological validity of data from this dataset, including the different slopes and the changes in direction during flat walking make our results more generalizable in classifying surfaces in real life conditions. 

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
There are four main limitations related to features, model architectures, populations sampled, and sensor set-up that warrant discussion and can be used to guide future work. 

First, in the ML model, we relied on generic (statistical- and frequency-based) features instead of domain specific-features. This may have hindered the model's ability to fully leverage the available gait information. This was done intentionally as we sought a fair comparison with the DL model. Incorporating (biomechanical) domain-specific features may provide the models with more discriminative information and improve their predictive performance. Also, we did not leverage advanced feature selection techniques (e.g. Salp Swarm Algorithm~\cite{mirjalili2017salp}) or feature extraction techniques that incorporate sensor fusion (e.g. Time Series Fusion (TSFuse)~\cite{de2024tsfuse}). The Salp Swarm Algorithm, as used in Chauhan et al.~\cite{chauhan2025classifying}, effectively reduces feature dimensionality while maintaining high accuracy. Though it is unclear whether Chauchan et al.~\cite{chauhan2025classifying} used a subject-wise split, making direct comparison with our results difficult. Moreover, we considered all our input data as univariate,  assuming no interaction between the signals. As we used a multiple sensor set-up, we might have missed information related to  the interactions of the different sensors. De Brabandere et al.~\cite{de2024tsfuse} developed an automated feature construction system (TSFuse) that fuses data from multivariate time series both within and between sensors, thereby creating new, and possibly relevant, time series~\cite{de2024tsfuse}. Future work could investigate the effect of these advanced and automated feature extraction tools to further refine model performance.
\textcolor{revision}{Additionally, calculating elevation changes between consecutive gait cycles or sliding windows may help disambiguate sloped from flat walking, thereby reducing misclassifications.}

Second, we only focused on two model architectures: a 1D CNN for the DL approach and XGBoost for the ML approach. The 1D CNN was chosen for its effectiveness in capturing local temporal patterns in sequential sensor data while maintaining a relatively low computational cost compared to more complex architectures like Long Short-Term Memory Units. Its structure is well-suited for processing multivariate time series such as IMU and pressure insole data, making it an efficient and interpretable choice for our classification task. XGBoost was selected for its strong performance with structured feature-based data, robustness to overfitting, and efficiency in handling high-dimensional input. Although we limited our exploration to these two well-established architectures to maintain a focused and interpretable evaluation, future work may benefit from exploring alternative or more advanced models tailored to specific sensor modalities.

Third, expanding surface classification studies to different populations remains a very important area for exploration. At this moment, studies have only used asymptomatic, uninjured subjects. While it is important to investigate the capabilities of classifying outdoor walking surfaces in unconstrained individuals, it will limit the generalizability of the models. It is unclear if models would misclassify surfaces due to an underlying gait pathology or when using walking aids. Therefore, future work needs to include participants with musculoskeletal and/or neurological injury for these models to be applicable for clinical biomechanics in free living environments.  

Lastly, our models depend on a complex sensor set-up. To achieve the best prediction F1-scores, we needed seven IMU sensors. \textcolor{revision}{Even though we achieved good (F1>0.9) accuracies for flat walking and stairs using IMU feet plus pressure insole models, classifying sloped walking remained challenging.} Future work should investigate methods to \textcolor{revision}{extract relevant features capable of recognizing sloped walking}, allowing for improved real-world applicability and feasibility.

In contrast, DL models achieved slightly better performance with sliding-window segmentation, which preserves temporal continuity well-suited for convolutional operations \cite{s25041275}. Regarding sensor configurations, the Luo setup which replaces foot sensors in the lower limb configuration with a right forearm sensor while retaining the pelvis, upper legs, and lower legs achieved performance comparable to the full lower limb configuration. This suggests that upper-limb motion may provide useful complementary information to lower-body kinematics, making the Luo configuration a practical alternative in situations where foot-mounted sensors are inconvenient or less feasible. In contrast, models relying solely on pressure insoles consistently underperformed, particularly in DL models, highlighting their limited capacity to capture full-body dynamics necessary for accurate walking condition classification.
